# **Purpose of a simulation?**

</div>

Modern simulation tools make it almost effortless to set up a model, run it, and process the outputs. Yet, many get stuck at the final step—interpreting the results, explaining their meaning, and deciding on the next course of action.

Simulations never reveal the complete reality; they merely produce solutions to the equations we define, shaped entirely by our inputs and assumptions.

A good way to approach simulations is to think of them as telling a story. 

### **Setting the Stage**

Every story begins with a reason for its existence, and every simulation must start with a strong problem statement.

Ask yourself: What problem am I solving with this simulation? 
What outputs am I expecting from it? This sets your purpose and frames the rest of the work. Clarify your thinking early. sometimes a quick calculation can clarify your thinking. If the starting point is weak, the rest of the story will struggle to convince.

### **Developing the Characters**

Characters are what drive a story forward. In the context of simulations, your characters are the models and inputs you provide. These must be logical, consistent, and as detailed as possible—because if your audience isn’t convinced by your setup, they won’t trust your results.

Take time to justify your choices, verify their plausibility, and ensure that everything aligns with the problem you are trying to solve. 

### **Navigating the Plot**

The plot is advanced by interactions, choices, and constraints—just as the solution method guides the model toward results. Ask yourself whether your solver is capable of handling the equations you’ve defined. Consider its level of accuracy, robustness, and ability to handle corner cases. Make sure you have the right inputs and all necessary data to actually solve the problem. If any of these points fail, you may need to revisit and adjust your model before moving forward.

### **Reaching the Climax**

A compelling climax brings all elements of a story together, revealing the total picture. In simulations, this moment happens in post-processing, when the raw outputs are transformed into meaningful insights. Here, it is critical to connect the dots between assumptions, methods, and results. Present them in a way that answers your initial problem statement convincingly. 

Engage with your target audience or stakeholders to understand what they want from the results, draw on previous studies for comparison, and test your model’s behavior using “what if” questions.

### **Closing the Story**

Finally, think about how you will present your findings. Whether it is a detailed report, a long presentation, a short slide deck, or even an email, the format should match the needs of your audience. Keep the context intact while adjusting depth and detail. 

The best stories leave room for sequels—so highlight areas left unexplored, explain why those gaps exist, and suggest possible directions for future work. A good simulation, like a good story, should leave the audiance both satisfied and curious.

Shared fictions and concepts are the foundational engine of human cooperation and knowledge development. These imagined stories provide the core framework that dictates how vast groups perceive reality and coordinate their actions. Ultimately, mastering this narrative architecture is what allows humans to construct, test, and transform complex social worlds.
